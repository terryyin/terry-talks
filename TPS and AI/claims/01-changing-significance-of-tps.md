# Claim 1: The changing significance of TPS

**Status: Provisional — not finalized**

## Original claim / hypothesis

TPS remains an important practical production system, management approach, and
philosophy within Toyota manufacturing, and its ideas remain influential in
manufacturing more broadly.

However, its wider economic significance may be smaller today than it was
around the 1980s–1990s.

TPS developed inside Toyota over many decades. During the period when
manufacturing and the production of physical goods were especially important
engines of economic growth in developed economies, Toyota's production
capabilities created enormous competitive advantage. TPS therefore mattered
not only to Toyota, but became an influential reference for manufacturing and
management internationally.

Today, a larger proportion of economic growth and competitive advantage in
developed economies comes from areas such as **innovation, knowledge work,
software, services, and the creation of new ideas**, rather than primarily from
improving the efficiency of physical manufacturing.

Toyota itself also needs capabilities for innovation that go beyond the
traditional scope of TPS.

The implication I initially drew was that TPS is still useful and actively
used, but is less significant today than it once was. Therefore, I should not
use TPS as a production system that modern software development should directly
imitate; instead, I should borrow relevant philosophy and principles from it.

## Research-based adjustment

The main conclusion still appears useful, but part of the reasoning needs to
change.

**TPS itself does not appear to have become substantially less important to
Toyota.** Toyota continues to describe TPS as a foundational capability or
“bedrock,” continues to practice kaizen around it, and applies TPS thinking
beyond conventional vehicle production.

A better distinction is:

> **TPS remains foundational to Toyota, but TPS alone is increasingly
> insufficient to describe all of the capabilities Toyota needs to compete.**

Toyota is simultaneously developing capabilities around software, vehicle
intelligence, electrification, experimentation, invention, mobility services,
and new businesses.

The historical importance of the 1980s–1990s also needs qualification. TPS had
already developed within Toyota for decades. What was particularly important
about the late 1980s and 1990s was that Toyota's production system became an
internationally recognized reference model, especially through the emergence
and popularization of **lean production**.

The economic argument should also be revised. It is too simplistic to describe
the change as:

> manufacturing drove economic growth then; innovation drives economic growth
> now.

**Manufacturing vs. services** and **production vs. innovation** are different
dimensions. Manufacturing can itself be highly innovative, while software and
services also require effective production systems.

A more defensible economic observation is:

> Developed economies have shifted strongly toward services and
> knowledge-intensive activities, while competitive advantage and productivity
> growth increasingly depend on innovation, technology, software, human
> knowledge, and other intangible assets.

This supports the software argument without requiring the stronger claim that
manufacturing is no longer economically important.

## Emerging implication for the talk

The reason not to transplant TPS directly into software should therefore
**not** be:

> TPS matters less because manufacturing matters less.

A stronger direction is:

> **TPS accumulated decades of learning about producing complex physical
> products repeatedly, with high quality, short lead time, and low waste.
> Software development—especially AI-augmented software development—has a
> significantly different production and discovery environment.**

Modern software development involves substantial uncertainty about **what
should be built**, while AI is dramatically reducing the cost and time required
to produce candidate implementations.

This suggests a potentially important contrast:

**TPS problem:** How can value flow rapidly while problems and defects are
exposed immediately?

**AI-augmented software problem:** What happens when generating another
candidate solution becomes extremely cheap and fast, while determining whether
it is **correct, useful, valuable, and appropriate** remains comparatively
expensive?

The direction for the talk may therefore be:

> Do not treat TPS as a recipe for software development. Examine the deeper
> reasoning and philosophy behind TPS, identify what problems those ideas were
> designed to solve, and ask which principles survive—or become even more
> important—when applied to AI-augmented knowledge work.

Toyota itself may provide an interesting case: it is attempting to retain TPS
as an operating foundation while simultaneously transforming into a
software-intensive mobility company.

## Questions still open

- How should “significance” be defined: importance inside Toyota, international
  management influence, contribution to economic performance, or relevance to
  the talk?
- What current, primary evidence best shows how Toyota describes the role of TPS
  in its mobility and software transformation?
- Which additional systems or philosophies is Toyota using for software,
  experimentation, invention, and new businesses?
- Can the difference between repeated physical production and software
  discovery be stated without creating an overly sharp manufacturing/software
  dichotomy?
- What evidence supports the claim that evaluating AI-generated candidates is
  becoming the comparative bottleneck?

**Still open for further discussion and fact-checking.**
