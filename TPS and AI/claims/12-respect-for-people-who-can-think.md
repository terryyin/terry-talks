# Claim 12: Respect for People is respect for people who can think

**Status: Provisional — people-as-thinkers reading supported; four-skill list
is Terry's mapping, not Toyota's**

## Original claim / hypothesis

> **TPS Respect for People is the realization that people who can think are
> the company's most important assets.**

The work itself demands and develops that thinking:

- JIT problem solving requires problem-solving skill.
- JIT collaboration requires facilitation skill.
- Kaizen needs analysis skill.
- Coaching needs coaching skill.

People who can do these things, and who keep acquiring more knowledge, are
the most important assets to the company.

This claim was split from [Claim 3](03-jidoka-enables-jit-trusts-respect-grows.md).
That claim already places mutual trust under Respect, growth under Teamwork,
and the failure mode that Respect alone does not guarantee growth. This claim
asks what the pillar is *for*.

## Research-based adjustment

The intuition is strongly supported. Toyota's own wording is close to Terry's
core, and weaker as a generic “people are our most important assets” slogan.

> **Respect for People is respect for people who can think. Jidoka and JIT
> work only through that capability. The work itself—real problems, stoppable
> abnormalities, kaizen, and teaching—is how the capability is grown.**

Fujio Cho states the philosophy that makes daily kaizen possible as Respect
for People: cultivate people so they can think on their own and improve the
workplace. That, he says, is what Toyota means by “Making things means making
people.” Toyota's current TPS account ends on the same commitment: develop
human resources who can think independently and implement kaizen. Only humans
can kaizen for the sake of evolution; machines, robots, and IT cannot evolve
further on their own. Human wisdom and ingenuity remain indispensable.

This is Terry's synthesis of what the pillar is *for*, not a Toyota slogan
that “people who can think are the company's most important assets.”

### Toyota names thinking people; it does not use the HR-asset slogan

Toyota Way 2001 places **Respect** and **Teamwork** under Respect for People.
Official reports define them as:

- **Respect:** understand others, take responsibility, and build mutual
  trust.
- **Teamwork:** stimulate personal and professional growth, share development
  opportunities, and maximize individual and team performance.

The same reports say Toyota aims to let people exercise their ability to
think, create, and take action, and that *monozukuri* (manufacturing) is
about developing people. Wisdom of people, used in constant improvement, is
what sustains growth. On-the-job training is the foundation.

The 1977 Toyota-authored account already called the companion to JIT a
**respect-for-human system**: workers display their capabilities in full by
participating in running and improving their own workshops. Authority to stop
the line, delegated shop-floor decisions, and the right and responsibility to
improve waste are part of that system—not a later HR overlay.

Akio Toyoda has called skilled workplaces among Toyota's **assets**. That is
the closest official use of asset language. The distinctive claim is still
not “invest in people.” It is:

> **The operating system requires people who can think, and the work is the
> school that makes them.**

A generic “people are our greatest asset” program can sit beside a system
that still uses people as labor to be utilized. Sugimori's contrast is
people displaying their **capabilities** by running and improving the work.
[Claim 3](03-jidoka-enables-jit-trusts-respect-grows.md) already records the
failure mode: without challenge, support, and real problems, Respect becomes
pressure rather than growth.

### The four skills are a software mapping, not Toyota's list

Toyota does not publish problem-solving, facilitation, analysis, and
coaching as the four Respect-for-People skills. The list is useful if it is
presented as what the work demands in this talk's language.

| Terry's skill | What the sources actually support |
|---|---|
| **Problem-solving** | Strongest. JIT removes hiding places; people must see a gap, respond, and prevent recurrence. Toyota's motto includes **Good Thinking, Good Products**. LeSS reports Eiji Toyoda telling managers to train people to think for themselves, and recasts managers as teachers of problem-solving rather than solvers on others' behalf. |
| **Analysis** | Present as genchi genbutsu, breaking down a problem, and kaizen of standardized work—not as a named “analysis skill.” |
| **Coaching** | Present as OJT, manager-teachers, and LeSS capability-building. “Coaching skill” is LeSS and later Kata language, not a Toyota Way keyword. |
| **Facilitation** | Weakest as a TPS name. Toyota's Teamwork is the official bucket. In this talk, the skill belongs with [Claim 3](03-jidoka-enables-jit-trusts-respect-grows.md)'s **Whole Product Focus**: integration conflict pulls collaboration. [Claim 9](09-nemawashi-self-organized-deliberation-in-less.md) asks how deliberation is prepared. Do not present facilitation as a Toyota skill list item. |

> **Name the skills if they help a software audience. Do not present the
> four-item list as Toyota's.**

### LeSS bets on the same kind of people

LeSS's people side matches this claim more tightly than a training budget
does. Self-managing feature teams decide how to work and how to improve.
Managers see the whole and **build capability**: they teach people to
improve and solve problems, and senior managers teach others to teach.
Value flows horizontally to the product; capability is built vertically.

That is why Whole Product Focus is not a substitute for thinking people.
Cross-component, customer-centric work is harder than a narrow specialist
queue. The teams can respond to an integration need only if they can
diagnose, collaborate, and improve the shared product. Technical excellence
in [Claim 8](08-technical-excellence-enables-jit-coordination-in-less.md)
makes that response cheap enough to pull; this claim is about the human
capability being pulled.

### AI can grow thinking capability or consume it

Toyota's recent monozukuri account keeps the same line after robots: even
as technology advances, humans equipped with TPS knowledge will think of
further improvements. [Claim 6](06-jidoka-embeds-routine-judgment.md)
places routine judgment in the process so adaptive attention can learn.
[Claim 10](10-freedom-and-trust-reinforce-through-jidoka.md) already records
the risk: AI can deskill, raise output targets, and leave people with only
the hardest exceptions.

This claim adds what must not be outsourced:

> **If generation is cheap, the scarce capability is still people who can
> judge usefulness, stop harm, improve the system, collaborate on the
> integrated product, and teach others to do the same.**

Using AI to skip the thinking is the opposite of Respect for People in this
sense. The work no longer develops the capability the pillars require.

## Emerging implication for the talk

Use Cho's wording rather than the asset slogan:

> **Cultivate people so they can think on their own and improve the
> workplace. Making things means making people.**

Then join it to [Claim 3](03-jidoka-enables-jit-trusts-respect-grows.md)'s
triad:

> **Jidoka frees attention. JIT entrusts a resourceful response. Respect
> for People is the bet that those people can think—and that giving them
> real problems, real collaboration, real kaizen, and real teaching is how
> the company gets more of them.**

For AI-augmented software development, the test is not whether people are
treated kindly while models generate. It is whether the system still
produces people who can think about the product. Keep problem-solving,
review, and teaching in the work. Saved time is capacity to learn, not
proof that thinking is no longer the job. Pull, stop, and deskilling
mechanisms are [Claims 4](04-jit-assurance-resourcefulness-and-constraint.md),
[6](06-jidoka-embeds-routine-judgment.md), and
[10](10-freedom-and-trust-reinforce-through-jidoka.md).

## Questions still open

- For the talk, is Cho's “think on their own / making things means making
  people” stronger than “people who can think are the most important
  assets”?
- Should the four-skill list stay as a software mnemonic, or collapse to
  **problem-solving and teaching others to solve problems**, with
  collaboration handled under Whole Product Focus?
- What one concrete story shows the difference from “invest in people”
  without becoming a second lecture on lean failures?
