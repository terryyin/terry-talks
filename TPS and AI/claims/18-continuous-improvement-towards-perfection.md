# Claim 18: Continuous improvement towards perfection is a LeSS principle from Toyota Way and lean thinking; SMED is a worked example

**Status: Provisional — LeSS principle and lineage sourced; SMED as
example is interpretive; not a finalized talk beat**

## Original claim / hypothesis

> LeSS has a principle: continuous improvement towards perfection. Is that
> also from TPS? SMED could be a good example of it.

This is a lightly edited record of Terry's hypothesis. It contains three
propositions:

1. **LeSS names it.** Continuous improvement towards perfection is a LeSS
   principle, not only a slogan.
2. **Origin.** The same idea may come from TPS—or from a nearby Toyota /
   lean layer.
3. **Example.** SMED can make the principle concrete: not a one-time
   setup-reduction project, but successive experiments toward an ideal
   that is never fully reached.

## Research-based adjustment

The first proposition is exact. The origin is Toyota, with the same
layer distinction as [Claim 2](02-tps-and-lean-houses.md) and
[Claim 16](16-go-see-ai-harness.md). SMED remains inside JIT; what it
illustrates here is the *trajectory*.

> **LeSS names Continuous Improvement Towards Perfection as a core
> principle and traces it to Toyota via lean thinking. Everyone
> implements daily kaizen and is never finished. Continuous Improvement
> is a Toyota Way pillar; “towards perfection” is how lean thinking and
> LeSS name the unattainable ideal that keeps that kaizen from becoming
> a change project with an end date. SMED is a worked example of that
> trajectory: successive experiments toward near-zero changeover so
> mixed-model flow, and cheap change of direction, become economical.**

### LeSS names the principle and says it comes from Toyota

LeSS lists **Continuous Improvement Towards Perfection** among its ten
core principles. The overview statement is:

> Create and deliver a product all the time, without defects, that
> utterly delights customers, improves the environment, and makes lives
> better. Do humble and radical improvement experiments each Sprint
> towards that.

The principle page contrasts a classic change program (“the change is
done”; settle into a new status quo; the next program undoes the last)
with LeSS: there is no change initiative, no change group, no change
managers. Change is the status quo. When is LeSS adoption done? Never.
When is improvement finished? Never.

LeSS itself locates the idea:

> Continuous improvement towards perfection is one of the two pillars of
> Lean Thinking, also a LeSS principle. Since lean thinking—and so
> continuous improvement—comes originally from Toyota, some of this
> introduction draws on the Toyota background.

So the talk can say **LeSS took this from Toyota via lean thinking.** It
should not say LeSS invented it.

LeSS also names **Lean Thinking** as a separate principle whose goal is
perfection. That is the house restated in
[Claim 2](02-tps-and-lean-houses.md): managers-as-teachers as foundation,
Respect for People and Continuous Improvement as pillars, all towards
the goal of perfection. This claim owns the *named* LeSS principle and
the SMED example, not the house diagram.

### TPS lives kaizen; the Toyota Way names the pillar; lean thinking names perfection

| Source | How it places the idea |
|---|---|
| **Toyota TPS overview** | The system is “the complete elimination of waste in pursuit of the most efficient methods.” All employees implement daily incremental kaizen; Toyota says it is still implementing kaizen to TPS “day and night.” Kaizen is how TPS is lived and evolved. |
| **Toyota Way 2001** | Two pillars: **Continuous Improvement** and **Respect for People**. “We are never satisfied with where we are.” Under Continuous Improvement: Challenge, Kaizen, and Genchi Genbutsu. [Claim 16](16-go-see-ai-harness.md) already owns Go-See / genchi genbutsu. |
| **Womack and Jones, *Lean Thinking*** | Five principles; the last is **pursue perfection**—repeatedly. Already compared in [Claim 7](07-the-algorithm-and-tps-family-resemblance.md). |
| **Larman/Vodde Lean Thinking House** | Continuous Improvement as a pillar; **perfection** as the goal / roof. [Claim 2](02-tps-and-lean-houses.md). |
| **LeSS** | A named principle, **Continuous Improvement Towards Perfection**, plus a Lean Thinking principle whose goal is perfection. Practices listed under the named principle include Go See, kaizen, value and waste, perfection challenge, work toward flow, and respect for people. |

The distinctive word is **towards**. Perfection is not a state to declare.
LeSS's examples of the ideal include: features that profoundly delight
customers; creating them almost without defect, effort, or cost;
organizational agility that can “turn on a dime for a dime”; extraordinary
breadth and depth of skill. None of those is a finish line. The
perfection challenge is high expectation and dissatisfaction with the
status quo, not a claim that Toyota or a LeSS group has arrived.

LeSS's adoption guidance makes the same point with a **perfection
vision**: an unattainable goal used to test whether a decision is
closer to the ideal. It gives Toyota's classic vision as Just-in-Time /
one-piece flow: every time a customer buys one car, exactly one car is
produced. The LeSS vision it sometimes uses is:

> Create the organizational ability to respond to changes by being able
> to deliver or change direction at any time without additional cost.

That is the software-shaped twin of cheap changeover.

### SMED is the trajectory, not a new home for the method

[Claim 4](04-jit-assurance-resourcefulness-and-constraint.md) already
has why it exists: even a known mix is made alternately; that is only
cheap if changeover is cheap. [Claim
5](05-smed-software-changeover-and-ai-friendly-context.md) already has
the software cut: cheap changeovers so customer-centric focus is not
interrupted, including accepted component and discipline switching
inside a slice.

What this claim adds is the *never-done* shape of that kaizen.

Shingo's method is staged, not a project with a completion certificate:
separate internal from external setup, convert internal work to external
wherever possible, then streamline what remains. The name targets a
single-digit number of minutes. One-touch exchange of dies (OTED) is a
further ideal: setup reduced toward a single step. Zero changeover is
the perfection the method points at and does not claim to have
finished. Remaining lots stay constraints under improvement
([Claim 4](04-jit-assurance-resourcefulness-and-constraint.md))—read
now as “towards perfection,” not as “good enough for this quarter.”

LeSS's principle page describes the same dynamic without naming SMED:

> This is a secret behind Toyota's efficiency—pull systems with small
> batches combined with kaizen drive new ways of working that lower the
> transaction cost of a process cycle.

High changeover cost is that transaction cost. Small batches look
wasteful until kaizen attacks it. Then mix leveling, vertical slices,
and frequent change of direction become the economical way to follow
actual need. That is why SMED is a good example of the principle, not
merely a setup trick under JIT.

The mapping to LeSS's own perfection pictures:

| Ideal LeSS names | SMED-shaped picture |
|---|---|
| Turn on a dime for a dime; change direction at any time without additional cost | Near-zero changeover; the product group can follow a new highest-value item without a large stranded batch or an organizational rebuild |
| Create the next increment easily, free of defects, with almost no extra effort | Externalized readiness and fast verification so starting the next item is not a reconstruction project |
| Extraordinary breadth of skill | Feature-team capability so the next item is not blocked by the only specialist |

Do not present SMED as how LeSS “implements” the principle. LeSS
implements it as endless inspect-and-adapt: master a baseline, run
experiments, make the better way the new temporary standard, repeat
forever. SMED is one historically vivid case of that loop applied to a
cost that otherwise forces large batches.

### What this claim does not own

- **Go-See** as firsthand facts, including inside an AI harness:
  [Claim 16](16-go-see-ai-harness.md). LeSS lists it under this
  principle; the talk should not re-teach it here.
- **Respect for People** as respect for people who can think:
  [Claim 12](12-respect-for-people-who-can-think.md).
- **One-piece flow / 一個ずつ確認:**
  [Claim 17](17-jit-vertical-slicing-one-piece-flow.md). LeSS treats
  work toward flow as a perfection challenge; the software cut of the
  flow is already that claim.
- **Jidoka** as stop-and-learn:
  [Claim 6](06-jidoka-embeds-routine-judgment.md). Kaizen embeds what
  stop-the-line discovers; it is not the same as lowering changeover
  cost.

## Emerging implication for the talk

After the TPS overview (jidoka and JIT) and the Toyota Way / LeSS house
(Claim 2), name this principle in LeSS's words so the audience hears a
familiar LeSS idea with its Toyota layer made explicit:

> **Never done. How Toyota lives jidoka and JIT, and how LeSS refuses
> to treat adoption as a project with an end date.**

Then make it concrete with SMED rather than with a second house lecture:

> **SMED is kaizen toward an ideal of cheap changeover. You do not finish
> setup reduction. You keep converting, streamlining, and challenging
> the leftover cost so mixed-model flow—and, in software, cheap change
> of direction—stays economical.**

The AI-era turn is the same “never done,” aimed at the leftover cost
Claim 5 already names. Generation of a candidate change is getting
cheaper. Reconstructing intent, preparing a safe environment, and
obtaining trustworthy feedback are still changeover. Launching more
agents does not substitute for that kaizen. Declaring “we have adopted
AI” is the change-program mistake LeSS is warning against.

> **When generation is cheap, the perfection vision is still cheap,
> trustworthy change of direction—not more unfinished parallel work.**

## Questions still open

- On stage, is the memorable label **Continuous Improvement Towards
  Perfection** (LeSS), **kaizen** (Toyota), or **never done**?
- Is SMED the strongest single example, or should the talk pair it with
  a LeSS experiment (Definition of Done expansion, build-time toward
  one second, abolishing a coordination role) so the audience sees the
  principle in product-development clothes?
- How much of Shingo's staged method belongs on stage once Claim 5 has
  already taught internal vs external setup?
- Does “turn on a dime for a dime” overpromise relative to LeSS
  protecting the Sprint goal? Claim 5 already distinguishes deliberate
  changeover from mid-Sprint interruption.
- Toyota Way 2020 still needs a primary check (open on Claim 2). Does
  that rewrite change how this principle should be attributed?
- The LeSS anecdote that the booklet was named *Toyota Way 2001* to
  mark “no final process”—is that in a Toyota-authored source the talk
  can cite, or only in LeSS?
